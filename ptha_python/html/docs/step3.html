3. Plate convergence

How fast the two plates move towards each other sets how many earthquakes a subduction zone can have. At a glance

Bird's model gives the plate speed along short pieces of plate boundary. Step 3 finds, for every column of cells, the nearest piece, turns its speeds into a convergent slip rate, and passes three results to the engine. Each result has one job.

Why the plate speed matters

At a subduction zone one tectonic plate slides under another (see PTHA in plain words). The convergence rate is how fast the two plates approach each other, in millimetres per year. Where the plates are stuck together at the fault, the motion is not free: the fault falls behind by that much every year, and an earthquake later makes up for it by slipping several metres at once.

This is the idea behind the moment balance (LEVEL 2 of the engine): over a long time, the total slip of all earthquakes must equal the slip the plates have accumulated. So the faster the convergence, the more (or the larger) earthquakes the zone must have. Only part of the motion is released by earthquakes; that fraction is the seismic coupling, which is uncertain and is a branch of the logic tree (LEVEL 1). The convergence itself is taken as known: it comes from a plate model.

Convergence also varies along a zone. On the kermadectonga2 mesh the convergent slip falls from about 250 mm/yr at Tonga (column 4) to about 6 mm/yr at the New Zealand end (columns 72 and 73; per column). PTHA18 uses that variation twice: to make ruptures more likely where the plates move faster, and to check that the modelled earthquakes release slip in the same along-zone pattern.

Bird's plate model

Bird (2003), "An updated digital model of plate boundaries" (the PB2002 model), divides every plate boundary on Earth into short straight steps (segments of a few tens of km) and gives, for each, the velocity of one plate relative to the other, split in two parts:

  • Div_vel, the divergent part, across the boundary, in mm/yr. Negative means the plates approach (convergence), positive means they separate.
  • RL_vel, the right-lateral part, along the boundary, in mm/yr: the sideways sliding. Its sign says the direction.

Which Bird table: --convergence bird or bird-griffin

The package holds two versions of Bird's model, both in ptha_python/data/bird/, and generate.py --convergence picks one. The default is bird. Nothing is read from rptha/ for this choice. Which PTHA18 zone used which table: Bird or Bird + Griffin, per zone.

Why the three convergent types. A subduction interface is a convergent boundary, but Bird does not always call it SUB. PTHA18 kept OCB steps where they carry the interface, as near Makran. Keeping only SUB would leave such zones with no match at all.

Class Normal rows of PTHA18's table are outer-rise traces: the bending of the plate before it sinks, where the sea floor is stretched, not squeezed. They carry a small positive Div_vel, so a column matched to one would get zero convergence. The R code skips them for a thrust zone by adding 1e12 m to their distance; the Python drops them. The public catalogue has no such rows.

What the choice changes. On PTHA18's own meshes (the table in data/bird/ptha18_convergence_sources.csv), of the 18 zones PTHA18 built from Bird's steps, the public catalogue gives exactly PTHA18's convergence on 8 (izumariana, kurilsjapan, newhebrides, southamerica, cascadia, ryuku, makran, philippine), is within 1.5% on 5 more (kermadectonga, kermadectonga2 +0.30%, alaskaaleutians, mexico, makran2 -1.34%), and differs by 4% to 138% on the other 5 (newhebrides2, sandwich, hjort, macquarieislandnorth, macquarienorth). The main cause is that PTHA18's table kept a few of Bird's steps of other types (transform faults, a spreading ridge), which the public catalogue leaves out (note 2). Where PTHA18 used Griffin's traces instead of Bird (eastern Indonesia and New Guinea) the two tables differ far more: newguinea2 gives 38.5 mm/yr with bird and 96.4 with bird-griffin (all zones).

Search box. Only Bird rows whose midpoint lies within 3 degrees of the mesh's cell centres are searched (bounding_box(..., margin_deg=3.0)). Fallback for bird-griffin. If PTHA18's table has no row left in the box (a zone PTHA18 never modelled), the step falls back to Bird's public catalogue for that zone and says so in the log.

Matching each column to Bird

The mesh is a grid of cells (unit sources): columns side by side along strike, each with a few rows down dip (row 1 touches the trench). Bird's boundary is drawn at the trench, so step 3 matches each column through its row-1 cell, as PTHA18's R code does (event_conditional_probability_bird2003_factory() in make_spatially_variable_source_zone_convergence_rates.R).

  1. Cell properties. Step 3 loads step 2's mesh and computes each cell's centre (centroid), strike, dip, length and width with unit_sources.discretized_source_approximate_summary_statistics(), the same table every later step uses.
  2. Keep row 1. Only the cells with downdip_number == 1 are matched, one per column. The code checks there is exactly one per column.
  3. Move to the top edge. The centroid is moved up dip by half the cell's width, projected on the map: destPoint(centroid, strike - 90, width/2 x 1000 x cos(dip)) on the WGS84 ellipsoid (geosphere's default in R, pyproj.Geod(ellps="WGS84") here). (top_edge_point())
  4. Bird midpoints. Each Bird step is represented by its great-circle midpoint, R's midPoint(p1, p2, f = 0) (a sphere). (spherical_midpoint())
  5. Nearest step. The distance from the top-edge point to every midpoint is computed with the haversine formula on a sphere of radius 6378137 m (R's distHaversine), and the smallest wins; with a tie, the first in table order (R's which.min). (dist_haversine())
  6. Copy down the column. The step's Div_vel and RL_vel become the column's; every cell of that column gets the same values.

Why these exact geodesy choices? To reproduce PTHA18's numbers. A plain average of the two ends' longitudes and latitudes, or another distance formula, picks a different step on a few columns. With the bird-griffin table, the area-weighted result equals PTHA18's to rounding error on PTHA18's own meshes (results).

From Bird's speeds to convergent slip

PTHA18's earthquakes are all pure thrusts (rake 90 degrees: the upper plate moves straight up the dip). The plates, however, rarely meet head-on. The rule of the PTHA18 R code (compute_rates_all_sources.R, lines 371 to 415) keeps the part that pushes across the trench and allows some of the sideways part, but not more than a slip direction 50 degrees away from pure thrust (rake_deviation = 50 in PTHA18's config.R):

div = max(0, -Div_vel)
rl = sign(RL_vel) x min( |RL_vel| , div x tan(50°) )
convergent slip = √( div² + rl² ) div: the convergent part in mm/yr; a diverging step (Div_vel > 0) gives 0. rl: the sideways part, capped; tan(50°) = 1.1918. For a normal-faulting source (rake -90) the first line is div = max(0, Div_vel). Any other rake is refused (convergence_components()).

Per column values and the zone's number

After the matching every column has two numbers: div, the convergent part only, and the convergent slip √(div² + rl²). Step 3 then computes one number for the whole zone, the area-weighted mean of the convergent slip over all cells:

convergence = Σ Ai si / Σ Ai Ai: area (length x width) of cell i; si: the convergent slip of cell i's column. Big cells count more. For kermadectonga2: 99.1568 mm/yr over 542,856 km² (data/convergence.txt holds the value with 10 decimals).

On kermadectonga2 the per-column values run from 5.8 mm/yr (columns 72 and 73, near New Zealand) to 251.4 mm/yr (column 4, at Tonga) for the convergent slip, and the div values from 4.4 to 229.0 mm/yr (column 5). The same values are drawn on the map by step 9 from data/convergence_per_cell.npy: every cell of a column has its column's colour, the three rows down dip sharing one value.

The two ways the engine uses the profiles

Profile 1, convergent slip: which scenarios are likely

Many earthquake scenarios share one magnitude, at different places along the zone. The engine splits each magnitude's rate among them (the conditional probability). With Bird's model a scenario's share is proportional to

its area x the area-weighted mean convergent slip of its cells

so ruptures on fast parts of the zone are more likely. In kermadectonga2 a rupture under the Tonga part (column 4 has the largest slip, 251.4 mm/yr) is more likely than one of the same size near New Zealand (5.8 mm/yr at column 72 or 73). With --rupture-size local the same weight is multiplied by the rupture-overlap factor q (rupture size).

run_logic_tree.py, branch cp_model == "convergent_slip_weighted"; R: make_conditional_probability_function_uniform_slip. Details: engine.

Profile 2, div: the LEVEL 4 target shape

LEVEL 4 (the edge correction) adds up, per column, the slip released by all scenarios and compares its shape along the zone with the shape of the plate motion. The target is div, not the convergent slip, and it is not weighted by area: the normalised shapes are compared directly.

Without a profile (constant-convergence zones) the target is flat: the same value in every column.

run_logic_tree.py, target_profile = rc.get("convergence_profile"); R: compute_rates_all_sources.R (div_vec). Details: LEVEL 4.

Step 6 writes both profiles into the input file's rates block through bird_convergence.rates_entries():

"conditional_probability_model": "convergent_slip_weighted",
"convergent_slip_profile": [177.35, ...],   # one value per column, mm/yr
"convergence_profile":     [114.0, ...],    # div per column

The engine spreads each list to every cell of its column and stops with an error if the list does not have one value per column (for example, if the mesh was rebuilt after step 3). (column_profile() in run_logic_tree.py.)

Why it mattersThe profiles do not change how many earthquakes of each magnitude happen; they change where the rate goes, and so which tsunamis carry it. With the flat target and "1/slip" weights used for constant-convergence zones, the per-scenario rates would differ from PTHA18's on the Bird zones. How the engine's rates compare with PTHA18's published rates is shown on the validation page.

The dip correction: once, in the engine

Bird's speeds are horizontal: how fast the plates approach on the map. An earthquake slips along the fault, which slopes down at the dip angle. To keep up with a horizontal approach v, the fault must slip v / cos(dip) along its slope (the long side of the triangle below).

How the engine does it. build_parameter_axes() in run_logic_tree.py builds one slip rate per coupling value:

slip rate (m/yr) = convergence / 1000 x coupling x 1 / cos(mean dip) convergence: tectonic_convergence_mm_per_yr from the input file, i.e. convergence.txt. mean dip: the angle mean of all cells' dips (the average of the unit vectors, as rptha's mean_angle does), moment_balance.mean_dip_cos_factor(); 15.4305 deg on kermadectonga2 (the plain average is 15.447). R: compute_rates_all_sources.R, sourcepar$slip = sourcepar$slip/cos_dip * 1/1000.

For example, with an illustrative coupling of 0.5 on kermadectonga2: 99.1568 / 1000 x 0.5 x 1.0374 = 0.0514 m/yr of slip on the fault.

How big the correction is. The moment balance makes the earthquake rates proportional to the slip rate, so the correction raises every rate before the update with observed earthquakes (LEVEL 3) by 1/cos(mean dip). The factor grows with the dip: on puysegur2, whose mean dip is 36.63 deg, it is 1.2461 (below).

Puysegur: a constant 35 mm/yr in PTHA18

PTHA18 used Bird on every zone except two: for puysegur and puysegur2 its parameter table (sourcezone_parameters.csv, column use_bird_convergence) says 0, and a constant 35 mm/yr from the literature was used instead. With a constant rate, the R model is also different: scenario probabilities follow the "1/slip" rule (inverse_slip) and the LEVEL 4 target is flat.

This package derives every input from public data, so by default it uses Bird on puysegur2 too: 25.60 mm/yr on its own mesh. On PTHA18's own mesh the public table gives 24.91 mm/yr (data/bird/ptha18_convergence_sources.csv). To reproduce PTHA18's choice instead, edit one line of the zone's step 3:

Does the mesh run past its own plate boundary?

The match above never fails. Every column is given the nearest Bird step, and "nearest" always returns something. So a mesh that extends beyond the end of its own subduction zone does not error: it quietly starts reading the next plate boundary's convergence, at a perfectly normal match distance. Nothing else in the run would show it.

Step 3 therefore checks for it on every run, and reports what it finds in the log and in report.html. It never trims anything by itself.

The case this check was built forkurilsjapan's SLAB raster continues south past the Japan Trench onto the Izu-Bonin Trench, a different plate pair that PTHA18 models separately as izumariana. A mesh built on the whole raster has 65 columns, and its last 5 sit on Izu-Bonin. They pull the zone convergence down to 86.18 mm/yr. Without them (--auto-clip, below) it is 87.91 mm/yr, against 87.72 mm/yr in PTHA18 (data/bird/ptha18_convergence_sources.csv).

What counts as a crossing

A step change in div between along-strike neighbours is the signature: within one boundary div drifts smoothly, while crossing onto another plate pair jumps it. Three conditions must all hold, and each one rejects a different false positive:

conditiondefaultwhat it rejects
jump between neighbouring columns≥ 25 mm/yrordinary drift along one boundary
sustained over a run of columns≥ 2a one-column spike that comes straight back: Bird's own segment noise
at an END of the zonewithin 25% of either enda mid-arc anomaly: a mesh cannot cross onto another plate in its middle and come back

The far side is compared against the median of the kept side, not its extremes: every suspect column must stay at least half the jump (12.5 mm/yr) beyond that median, so a column that comes straight back is not a crossing. A single within-zone dip among neighbours is ordinary Bird noise and must not veto a real crossing a few columns away. Meshes with fewer than 6 columns are not checked. If several jumps qualify, the largest one is reported. The three thresholds are the constants PLATE_CHANGE_JUMP_MM_PER_YR, PLATE_CHANGE_MIN_RUN and PLATE_CHANGE_END_FRACTION in lib/bird_convergence.py.
